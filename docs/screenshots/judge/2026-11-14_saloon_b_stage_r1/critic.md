# Blind critic, 2026-11-14_saloon_b_stage_r1 (saloon B's stage)

Seen: a.png (curtain closed, from the hall), b.png (curtain open, backdrop), c.png (beside the stage, looking back into the hall). Reference: variety stages in Western dance halls and opera houses of c. 1875-1885 (photographs and accounts): a painted or moulded proscenium with a fringed valance, a row of footlights in a sunken trough behind a low hood, a painted roll drop or tied-back drapes, a perspective scene drop with wings.

## The five biggest things

### 1. The footlights are five big tin buckets, and only two are lit
- **Where:** a, `420 390 440 110` (crop `a_footlights.png`; also in b at the same place).
- **What:** five oversized cans stand on the stage lip like ice buckets. Two shine white and throw hard conical hot spots straight up the curtain; the other three are dark. The apron below them gets almost no light.
- **Why it reads wrong:** footlights of the period were a continuous row of many small oil (or gas) burners with tin reflectors, set in a trough along the front of the stage behind a low hood, so the house saw a glow rather than the fixtures. All of them burned together and gave an even, low, warm wash from below. Five large lamps, alternately lit, read as modern can lights and draw the eye away from the stage.
- **Cheapest fix:** replace them with 8 to 12 small lamps (a chimney and a reflector each) sunk behind a plain board hood along the lip, all lit. Lower each one's energy so they add up to one soft wash across the bottom third of the curtain, with no hot spots.

### 2. The proscenium is a bare dark box: no valance, no decoration, no painted apron
- **Where:** a, `400 180 500 340` (crop `a_proscenium.png`).
- **What:** a plain rectangular frame of dark board, a flat-topped opening, and a stage front of plain planks with two thin brass lines. The curtain hangs straight to the deck from a track like a modern traveler.
- **Why it reads wrong:** even rough frontier halls dressed their stage. They had a scalloped, fringed valance (lambrequin) across the top, a painted or moulded frame (cream and gilt, pilasters each side, often a lyre, a mask or the hall's name on a panel over the opening), and a painted apron front. The act curtain was usually a painted roll drop, or drapes swagged and tied back. As it stands it looks like a box built to hold a curtain, not a little theatre, and the dark frame merges into the dark rafters.
- **Cheapest fix:** add a fringed swag valance across the top of the opening, paint the frame a light colour with a simple pilaster each side and a lettered or painted panel above, and give the apron a painted panel or a motto. Tie-backs on the curtain when it is open (b) would finish it.

### 3. The curtain looks like orange plastic tubes
- **Where:** a, `420 270 440 170`; c, `300 180 200 300` (crops `a_proscenium.png`, `c_stage_side.png`).
- **What:** the pleats are perfectly even, rigid half-cylinders of a very saturated orange-red that almost glows. The gold hem is a row of separate blocky squares, one per pleat. From the side (c) each pleat is a clean cylinder, like a row of pipes.
- **Why it reads wrong:** wool rep or velvet stage curtains were a deep crimson or maroon, heavy and soft, with folds of varying depth, dark troughs and a soft sheen along the crests, and a bullion fringe or tasselled hem. Under lamplight they look dark and rich, not bright orange.
- **Cheapest fix:** darken and desaturate the albedo toward crimson; vary the pleat depth and spacing a little; raise roughness with a velvet-like sheen on the crests; make the hem one continuous fringe band rather than a block per pleat.

### 4. The backdrop is a framed booster lithograph, not a stage scene
- **Where:** b, `460 225 370 200` (crop `b_backdrop.png`).
- **What:** a bird's-eye view of the town, from high above, with a banner reading "SALT CREEK, The Garden of the Valley", a thin border all round like a framed print, and a dark gap between it and the proscenium above and at the sides. The red legs either side are narrow strips. There is a dark vertical crease or stain down the middle.
- **Why it reads wrong:** this is the look of a county-atlas or real-estate lithograph, or a framed advertisement in the bar, not of scenery. A drop was painted broadly in distemper as a perspective scene at the actors' eye level (a wood, a street, a parlour, a garden), filled the whole opening from edge to edge with no border, and usually had a pair of painted wings for depth. A banner title belongs on the act drop, if anywhere. Here the drop looks small and far back, like a picture hung on a wall behind the stage.
- **Cheapest fix:** scale the drop so it fills the opening and runs behind the legs (lose the border); repaint, or pick, an eye-level scene with a low horizon; move or drop the banner; add one pair of plain painted wing flats just inside the proscenium.

### 5. The side of the stage is a giant plywood slab
- **Where:** c, `0 180 560 540` (crop `c_stage_side.png`).
- **What:** the large panel at the left of c (the stage's side wall or proscenium return) has huge flame-figured "cathedral" grain, sliced like modern rotary-cut plywood or furniture veneer, at about five times the scale of real grain. Next to it the stage's front riser is a pale, unpainted strip that does not match anything around it.
- **Why it reads wrong:** an 1880s hall's stage was built of narrow sawn boards (painted, or plain like the hall's own walls); a single sheet of figured wood this size did not exist in a frontier building, and the grain scale makes the stage look like a toy. It is the first thing a viewer sees in c.
- **Cheapest fix:** give the panel the same narrow vertical boards as the hall walls (or paint it with the proscenium's colour) at true grain scale; paint or stain the riser to match the apron.

**Also noticed:** the hall itself is bare: no benches or chairs along the walls, no musicians' place, no hanging lamps or chandelier over the floor, one sconce on the left wall (a, `0 160 420 360`, crop `a_hall_left.png`). A dance hall of the time had a bench line round the walls, a bar or drinks table, and lamps hung from the tie beams. The whitewashed boards' speckle reads as noise more than as wear.

## What already works
- The open-rafter roof with heavy tie beams and the plank floor give a believable timber upper hall.
- The warm, low lamplight and deep shadows in the corners are the right mood for an evening hall.
- The stage's place at one end of the hall, raised and with steps at the side (c), is the right arrangement.
- The stair rail and balusters on the left of a and b read as period.

## Outright faults
- c, `250 600 140 120` (crop `c_flame.png`): a bare flame and its glow at the foot of the side panel with no lamp, chimney or holder visible: it floats. A similar clipped glow sits at the left edge of c around `0 230 30 60`.
- a and b: three of the five footlights are dark while two blaze; if all are meant to be lit, some are not.
- b: a dark gap between the top of the backdrop and the proscenium header shows the drop is hung short of the opening.
