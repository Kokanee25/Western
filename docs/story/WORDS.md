# Words: how people talk in Salt Creek, 1882

The story bible, part 4 of 5 (#809). Planning only. Period words to use, modern words to avoid, and
how each kind of person in the valley would really talk. Written for the scripts (#807): every line
is checked against this before it's voiced.

**How far to trust it.** A date marked **✓** was looked up for this file on 2026-11-11 and its
source is linked at the end. Everything else comes from the standard references named there, from
memory, and is marked **ref**: look it up before a script leans on it (Green's Dictionary of Slang
is free online and dates every sense). Where two sources disagreed, both are given. No paid tools
were used.

## Seven rules

1. **Period words carry it, not spelling.** One "ain't" or a dropped g places a man. Don't write
   dialect out letter by letter ("Ah reckon yuh"): the voice actor, or the voice model, does the
   accent.
2. **Short.** A cowhand says seven words where a modern script says twenty. The educated (the doctor,
   the Baron, Rourke) are marked by whole sentences, not by long words.
3. **Swearing is blasphemy, not sex.** "Damn", "hell", "God damn", "Jesus", "son of a bitch". The
   modern all-purpose words were not used as punctuation in 1882 print or, as far as anyone can show,
   in speech. Women are not sworn in front of, and men notice when someone does it.
4. **Titles matter.** "Mr.", "Mrs.", "ma'am", "sir", "Doc", "Sheriff", "Deputy". First names are for
   kin, old friends, children, and men you look down on.
5. **Understate.** "He's poorly" for dying. "A little trouble" for a killing. "I'd as soon not."
6. **If a word feels like a Western, check it.** Most of the famous ones are from films and the
   pulps of the 1920s on (see "Avoid").
7. **Slurs: a few, from the worst men only, at moments that matter** (D, Sean 2026-11-11, #830, C13
   b). The men: Kett, Cody, the loudest man at the rope. Never from anyone else: not Harlan, not the
   journal or the paper, not anyone the game wants liked. How to apply it (P·bible): each one is a
   moment the script names and says why it's there (a man showing what he is, a crowd turning);
   never as colour or as a habit of speech, and never twice where once will do. Everyone else carries contempt as the period did
   without the words: "your Indian", "that Ute", "them", and what men do. The words themselves are
   chosen in the script, not listed here. Whether the voice service will say them, and how the store
   page warns of them, is O (some lines may not be voiceable).

**Modern speech that slips in** (no date needed; if a line has one of these, rewrite the line):

- "okay?", "no problem", "I'm good", "I'm fine", "you all right?" → "all right", "it's nothing",
  "tolerable", "you hurt?"
- "guys", "kid" for a grown man, "buddy", "boss" as a greeting → "boys", "son", "friend", "pard"
- "deal with it", "handle it", "take care of him" (to kill) → "see to it", "settle him"
- "issue", "problem" (for trouble), "situation", "scenario" → "trouble", "a fix", "the matter"
- "contact", "reach out", "check on", "figure out" → "send word", "look in on", "make out"
- "sure thing", "absolutely", "exactly", "seriously", "literally", "basically", "whatever"
- "stressed", "upset" (for grief), "trauma", "closure", "process it", "paranoid", "I feel like"
- "back off", "calm down", "relax", "get over it" → "stand away", "easy now", "let it lie"
- "What's your problem?", "You've got to be kidding", "We need to talk", "Listen to me"
- "weekend", "teenager", "lifestyle", "deadline", "backup", "feedback", "scientist" for the doctor

## How the people of the valley talk

### Cowhands (the Baron's riders, the Colters, Kett's crew, the foreman)
The trade came up from Texas, so the talk is Texan with Spanish in it: "lariat", "remuda", "savvy",
"hombre", "loco", "calaboose", "vamoose", "pronto" (ref). Flat, slow, dry; a joke is said without a
smile. Measures things by work: "a two-day ride", "forty a month". A hand "rides for the brand".
Hard words for homesteaders ("nester", "granger"). Among themselves, nicknames by place or look
("Dutch", "the Kid", "Tex"), which is why the town knows Kett only as "the red shirt". The primary
books to steal rhythm from: Charles Siringo's *A Texas Cow Boy* (1885) and Andy Adams's *The Log of a
Cowboy* (1903, about a drive in 1882); the word list is Ramon Adams's *Western Words* (ref).

### The doctor
An educated man: whole sentences, no slang, and the medicine of 1882. "The ball", "the probe",
"lint", "carbolic", "laudanum", "morphia", "chloroform", "a ligature", "a tourniquet",
"mortification", "the bowel is cut", "shock", "lung fever", "consumption" (ref). He says "I can do
nothing" where a modern doctor says "I'm sorry". Antisepsis was only partly taken up (DESIGN.md §6),
so "clean" is his own habit, not everyone's. He calls Harlan's old rank by its name: "hospital
steward".

### The Baron
Marked by courtesy and length, not by slang. "I should be obliged." "My figure." "A consideration."
"Title", "the range", "improvements", "prior right". He says "ma'am" and "sir" to people he is
ruining. Many of the big cattle outfits of the 1880s were Eastern or British money (ref), so where
he's from, and whether he has an accent, is Sean's call. He never says "I'll kill you". He says
"That would be a pity."

### A Southern Ute man (Joe)
This is the one to get right, and the one most often done wrong.
- **Who his people are.** In the 1880s the Southern Utes were three bands, the Mouache, the Capote
  and the Weeminuche, on a strip of reservation along Colorado's southern edge ✓. The Ute name for
  themselves is written "Nuche" by a local museum ✓; spellings vary, and the tribe's own is the one
  to use. In 1881 it was the White River and Uncompahgre Utes who were removed to Utah; the southern
  bands stayed (`i-am-the-law`, checked there).
- **His languages.** Ute first. Then Spanish: the southern bands had traded with New Mexico's
  settlements for more than two centuries, and the two peoples knew each other's languages ✓. English is his
  third, picked up at the agency, the railroad and the town.
- **So his English is** exact and plain, with few contractions and no slang he hasn't heard. It isn't
  broken, and it isn't grand. He says a thing once. He may reach for a Spanish word before an English
  one.
- **Never:** "heap", "many moons", "paleface", "firewater", "great father", "how", dropped verbs ("me
  go"), or speeches about the land. He is a man whose wife was murdered, not a symbol.
- **His name.** The Ute leaders of 1882 are in the record under names their neighbours and the
  agency used: Ignacio, Severo, Buckskin Charley ✓. "Joe" is that kind of name. His own is not ours
  to make up (`VOICES.md`, "Names").
- **What others call his people:** "the Utes", "the Indians", "the agency Indians"; the decent ones
  say "Joe's people". The worst men's words: rule 7 (C13 b).
- **Ute in the game** (the camp's talk, a word of Joe's to his dead) is written with a Southern Ute
  speaker or not at all.

### A railroad man (the depot agent, the section foreman, the engineer, the express messenger)
Exact about time and place: "four minutes down", "two rail lengths west of the tank", "Number
Three". Says "the cars", "a special", "orders", "the wire", "on the ground" (derailed), "in the
ditch", "pull the pin" (link-and-pin couplers), "set the brakes", "the tank" (ref). Calls the track
gang "section hands" and their boss the "section foreman" or "section boss". The engineer is "the
engineer"; the fireman "the fireman" (the nicknames "hogger" and "tallow pot" are in every list of
railroad slang ✓ but none of the lists dates them: to check). **Railroad time is not town time:**
standard time came in November 1883, so the depot's clock and the town's differ by minutes, and the
agent goes by his (#789).

### The town's women (the widow, the hotel's landlady, the storekeeper's wife, the women upstairs)
They talk as plainly as the men and with better grammar: schooling was women's work. "Mr. Harlan",
never his first name. "I'll thank you to..." is a warning. They speak of bodies and births
sideways ("in a family way", "her time", "poorly") and of money and land straight. A widow is "Mrs."
and her husband's name for life. The women who work upstairs at the saloon are "the girls" to the
barkeep and "those women" to the town; none of them is a joke (DESIGN.md §5: "people, not prizes").
A woman swears rarely, and the room stops when she does.

### Miners
"Giant", "powder", "caps and fuse", "a shot", "a round", "the face", "the drift", "the adit",
"timber" and "sets", "muck", "single jack" and "double jack" (hammers), "bad air" (ref). After a shot
the danger is "powder smoke" and "bad air", not the firedamp of coal mines (to check; s16). Many
Colorado hard-rock men were Cornish, "Cousin Jacks" (ref): if the miner is one, a "my son" or two is
enough.

### Soldiers (Rourke, the captain, the surgeon)
"Sir." "The post." "The department." "A detail." "My orderly." "Regular" for proper. "Citizens" for
civilians. Rank is always said. An officer is never loud. The surgeon's men in 1882 were a hospital
steward and privates detailed to him (`payday-night`, checked there).

## To check before anything is recorded

1. "gut-shot", "out cold", "hold it", "fire in the hole", "nester", "dry-gulch", "hello": dates.
2. "hogger", "tallow pot", "varnish": are they 1880s, or later railroad talk?
3. The Ute name for the people, as the Southern Ute Tribe writes it.
4. C12: the 1877 Colorado law that has the coroner act as sheriff when a county has none ✓ (as
   reported), read in the 1877 General Laws themselves.
5. C8: who tried a white man for a crime against a Ute on the reservation in 1882. *United States v.
   McBratney* (1881) ✓ took white-on-white crimes there away from the federal court, and is read as
   leaving crimes against Indians with it.
