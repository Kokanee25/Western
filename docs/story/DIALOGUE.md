# Dialogue: how people talk in Salt Creek

The story bible's house style for spoken lines (#1056, 2026-11-17). **This is the one place for how
people talk.** The words themselves are in [`WORDS.md`](WORDS.md); each man's own voice is in
[`VOICES.md`](VOICES.md); what a line may know or pay off is in [`OUTLINE.md`](OUTLINE.md) and
[`SETUPS.md`](SETUPS.md). A script (`docs/scripts/`) is checked against all of them, and then read
blind (the last section).

Sean, 2026-11-17: the lines must read as real people, "realistic and as entertaining as possible",
and not as AI-written.

## 1. What the period's own record shows

Read for this file on 2026-11-17, from public-domain texts (the list and links are at the end).
Fiction by men who were there (Twain, Harte, Adams, Siringo), and the non-fiction record: sworn
testimony, newspapers, a traveller's letters, a diary, a surgeon's paper, three slang dictionaries of
1872 to 1889. Quotations are as printed; the dashes are the printers'.

**Before a shooting, nobody makes a speech.** At Tombstone in 1881 the witnesses agree on what was
said: an order and an oath, then firing. "'Throw up your hands,' or, 'Give up your arms,' and then
the shooting commenced." "You S—— of ——, you have been looking for a fight and you can have it."
The men shot at said plainer things still: "Don't shoot me, I don't want to fight." "I have got
nothing." "They have murdered me." Jack McCall, on trial at Deadwood in 1876: "Wild Bill killed my
brother and I killed him."

**Courtesy is the dangerous register.** Twain's Slade is "so friendly and so gentle-spoken"; Siringo
has Billy the Kid say "Hello, Bob!" "in a pleasant voice" before he fires. A Texas Ranger takes a
rustler's pistol with "Come here, dear, and give me that gun of yours." A cook: "Will you please go
to hell?" A trail foreman ends a quarrel over cattle: "My Christian friend, there's no use arguing
this matter." The tenser it gets, the more "gentlemen", "Mr." and "friend". That is the stranger
at Harlan's table, and it is why the sheriff let him ride up.

**Oaths are blasphemy, and print hides the worst.** "d—d", "s—— of a b——", "God damn". Twain says
of one printable insult that the word really used was "too strong for print". Dimsdale (Montana,
1865) says that to call a man "a liar, a thief, or a son of a b——h" was held reason enough to kill
him on the spot: the phrase is strong, and a man who shouts it means to fight. The mild oaths are
odd and personal: "dern my skin", "Hell's fire and little fishes!", "I wish I may rot in my tracks."
Rough men apologise for swearing in front of a parson ("Beg your pardon, friend, for coming so near
saying a cuss-word") and are expected not to before women: Isabella Bird notes a stage driver who
"never spoke without an oath, and though two ladies were passengers", because it was worth noting.

**Death and trouble are said sideways, or dead flat.** "One of the boys has passed in his checks."
"A little unpleasantness occurred in the Oriental Saloon yesterday." Or with no comment at all: "a
plough handle kicked him in the side and we buried him within a week." Harte's Mother Shipton,
starving: "I'm going, but don't say anything about it. Don't waken the kids." Nobody says how they
feel about it.

**A man comes at the thing from the side.** Tennessee's Partner walks into his friend's murder
trial: "I was passin' by, and I thought I'd just step in and see how things was gittin' on with
Tennessee thar, my pardner." Questions get a question back ("Trial! What do I want to try him for?").

**A man's trade gives him his figures of speech.** Twain's Scotty Briggs talks cards to a parson and
neither understands the other ("Are you the duck that runs the gospel-mill next door?" "I am the
shepherd in charge of the flock whose fold is next door." "The which?"). The gambler deals; the
cowhand counts in cattle and miles. Nobody reaches for a general truth.

**People under oath hedge the words and are sure of the order.** "I think, though I am not positive…
or words to the same effect." "A man I suppose to have been Billy Clanton." Their grammar slips and
their diction is formal: "All three cowboys done so, and then the shooting commenced."

**Wounds are places on the body.** "Shot in the belly." "Below the right nipple." "A slight
flesh-wound." A Tombstone surgeon, of a man shot in the bowel: "never rallied, dying from shock
about eighteen hours afterward." No witness says "gut-shot" (`WORDS.md`, the checks).

**Humour is deadpan, and offered as fact.** "If your mate would like to come to the hanging, fetch
him along. I like him." The Dodge City Times on a policeman: he "gently tapped belligerent Shaw upon
the head with his shooting iron, merely to convince him of the vanities of this frail world."
Nobody in these books says a clever thing and waits for the laugh.

**The educated and the rough don't meet in the middle.** The educated speak in long, complete
periods; the rough in fragments ("Scasely." "The which?"). Neither translates for the other.
Adams's cowhands, though, speak in nearly plain whole sentences: the heavy dialect spelling is
Twain's and Harte's miners. Women speak plainly and practically and give their trade first ("I am a
dressmaker").

**How Colorado talked about the Utes in 1879–80.** The Denver press was openly hostile ("red
devils", "savages", sarcasm about the "noble red man"), and a town reads its paper. Sworn witnesses
said "the Indians" and named men. Bird, an outsider, blamed the agency. So the town's first guess on
night one is "Utes" (`SETUPS.md` S37), and the slurs stay with the three men of `WORDS.md` rule 7.

**What the films added.** "Howdy" is in none of the four books: men say "Hello", "How're ye", "How
goes it?", "So long", "Adios". Nor are "gunslinger", "showdown", "tarnation" (a Yankee word),
"y'all" or "reach for the sky": the order was "Throw up your hands." "Hold-up" was "new" to a
dictionary of 1889, and "rustler" first meant a hustling, capable man. "Reckon" is not universal:
Bird's Colorado settler says "guess". In Dodge a man left his pistol at the first place he stopped.
Lawmen kept saloons.

## 2. The craft

1. **Short.** One breath. Two sentences is a long line; three is a speech, and only the Baron,
   Rourke and the preacher make speeches.
2. **Every speaker wants something in the scene, and it is rarely what the scene is about.** The
   barkeep wants his chair and his four dollars. The stranger wants to know if the sheriff could
   talk. The doctor wants to get back to his patient. Write the want, not the theme.
3. **Say less than he means.** The widow to the Baron at the grave: "He's not covered over yet."
   Joe on the ride home: "Her mother will ask me is it finished. I will tell her yes."
4. **People talk past each other, interrupt, and answer the wrong question.** The sheriff, dying,
   over the doctor's whisper at the door: "I can hear you, Doc. Send him in."
5. **Humour comes from who he is**, never from a joke the writer had. The barkeep counts cost, so a
   dead man owes him four dollars. Kett has been in the state prison, so he rates a jail by its
   supper. The undertaker is exact, so the dead man's estate is "a deck of cards and one boot". If a
   line would be as funny in another mouth, it's the writer's and it goes.
6. **Concrete nouns.** Dogs, bread, a dime on the bar, a hat won at monte. Not grief, justice, the
   law. The titles (No Law, No Justice, I Am the Law) may be said by a character once each.
7. **A line must survive the player.** He can walk off, shoot, or stand there (DESIGN.md: acts
   "hand back to the systems the moment you interfere"). So no line needs the one before it to make
   sense, no line needs an answer, and nothing important is said only once in one place (`SETUPS.md`
   rule 1). A line that can repeat has two or three wordings.
8. **Harlan doesn't speak.** He nods, shakes, or acts. So people ask him things a nod can answer, and
   they notice ("Doc, you saw that. He's sworn.").
9. **The body changes the words, not only the voice** (DESIGN.md signature 1). A weak man is short
   of breath, not quiet: fewer words, still voiced. Sean's notes on the sheriff's takes
   (2026-11-13) were nearly all "too much whisper": if a line reads as a whisper, re-word it until
   it can't be said that way.
10. **The town remembers.** A line that only plays when a fact is true (`when`, a StoryState fact)
    beats a line that plays for everyone. What the player did on the road, at the table, at the
    oath and at the cabin is known by someone in every scene after (`SETUPS.md` part E).
11. **Each man keeps his own sound.** One line of each, side by side, should be tellable apart with
    the names covered. The sheriff gives orders. The doctor counts. The stranger asks leave. The
    Baron's sentences are long and never finish with a threat. Joe is plain and practical and talks
    about things, not about what they mean. Kett sells. Cody shouts. The barkeep is owed.

## 3. The AI tells: banned, by name

A line with one of these is rewritten.

1. **A character naming his own feelings**, or the meaning of the moment. ("It did not give her
   back. I knew it would not." "I haven't decided what I think.")
2. **Tidy threes**, and the button: a last short sentence that closes the line like a lid. ("That
   is all they saw of him. It is enough." "Not yet." "Nobody could.")
3. **Everyone equally witty, in one rhythm.** A line that could be swapped to another speaker. The
   dry clipped tag is the commonest: if the doctor, the undertaker and the stranger all end on one,
   two of them lose it.
4. **Explaining what the scene already shows.** ("He talks a great deal for a man with his hands
   tied." "Doc's right. No jury in Colorado'll hang a white man for a Ute woman.")
5. **Polished symmetry**: balanced pairs, "Not X. Y.", the made-up proverb. ("Not today. Not any
   day." "You hear that word off a stranger, look at his horse.")
6. **Modern idioms and therapy-speak** (`WORDS.md`, "Avoid"), and **the film Western**: "We got
   company!", "I ain't dead yet", "First man through that door gets it", the one-word menace.
7. **"A testament to", "in a world where" and their kin**: any abstraction a man in a saloon
   wouldn't say.
8. **Every line a complete grammatical sentence.** Real talk has fragments, false starts and
   sentences left hanging.
9. **The stately Indian.** No "Then we go now", no present tense for everything, no contraction-free
   solemnity. Joe's English is his third language: plain, exact, practical, with the contractions
   he has heard a thousand times (`WORDS.md`, "A Southern Ute man").
10. **The quip in the gunfight, and the dying man's epigram.** A man with a ball in his belly
    doesn't turn a phrase.

## 4. The blind read-through

Before a script goes to Sean: a fresh sub-agent that has seen none of the work gets **only** section
3 and the script files, and flags every wording that sounds modern, AI-written, flat or samey, with
why; it also names the speakers who sound most alike and the lines to keep. Fix what it flags. At
most two rounds (CLAUDE.md). Lines that are Sean's own or fixed in the game are not changed: they go
in the script's `kept` list with the reader's objection, for Sean.
