# Brief: the game's booze (#1222)

Invented brands for everything drunk in Salt Creek, 1882, and the labels, bottles and barrel heads
that carry them. Prop lab. Steps 1-3 (research, names, a blind check) are this file; step 4 (the
labels as textures, the bottle shapes, placing them) waits for Sean's read of the table.

**Rules for a name.** Invented: never a real brand of the period or of today (each was checked, see
"The blind check"). In the game's voice (`docs/story/WORDS.md`): proud distillers, a little humbug,
and jokes the label itself doesn't know it is making. Nothing modern ("craft", "small batch",
"premium", "smooth"). No Indian chiefs, maidens or "Indian cure" bitters, though the period was full
of them: the Utes are real people in this story (DESIGN.md §5, "History, handled with care").

## 1. What was drunk in an 1882 Colorado mining and railroad town

How far to trust it: from memory and the standard references (Wilson's *Spirits Bottles of the Old
West*, Ring and Ham's *Bitters Bottles*, Fike's *The Bottle Book*, the SHA bottle-dating site), not
looked up this session. Marked **ref**: check before a label leans on a date.

- **Whiskey is most of it.** Bourbon and rye came west by rail in barrels, from Kentucky,
  Pennsylvania (Monongahela rye) and the big wholesalers and **rectifiers** of St. Louis, Cincinnati
  and Denver, who blended straight whiskey with neutral spirit, colouring and flavour, and sold it
  under their own names. A saloon drew from the barrel or refilled bottles that had once held
  something better (ref). The cheapest was **trade whiskey**: "tanglefoot", "forty-rod", "red-eye",
  "Taos lightning" are what men called it, not brands. A barrel head carried the distillery's burned
  or stencilled name and the gauger's stencil (serial, proof gallons, distillery and district
  number, date) by the revenue laws of the 1870s (ref). **No strip stamps over the cork: they're
  1897, Bottled-in-Bond.**
- **Beer: mostly by the keg** from a local or Denver brewery (Colorado had dozens of small
  breweries by 1880, ref). **Bottled lager** was new and still a little fine: Pasteur's heating let
  the St. Louis and Milwaukee brewers ship "export" lager by rail from the late 1870s (ref). Corked
  and wired, or a lightning (wire bail) stopper; **no crown caps (1892)**. English ale and porter at
  the hotel, imported.
- **Bitters**: patent "stomach bitters", 25-45% alcohol (sold as 50-90 proof "medicine"), the
  biggest-selling patent medicine of the age, untaxed as liquor, drunk by the glass in saloons and
  by the bottle from the store by men and women who wouldn't be seen with whiskey. The labels list
  every complaint known; "the alcohol is only a preservative" is a real line of the time (ref).
- **Gin**: Holland gin (Geneva, Schiedam) in square, tapering olive-green "case" bottles, sold as a
  kidney medicine as much as a drink; Old Tom (sweetened, English) at the hotel, with the cat on
  the label (ref). **Rum**: New England rum, little of it, winter toddies. **Brandy**:
  California grape brandy (cheap, and grown by 1880), French cognac at the hotel; blackberry brandy
  for "summer complaint". **Wine**: champagne and claret only for the hotel and the rich.
- **Soft**: sarsaparilla (sold as a blood medicine, some of it with alcohol), ginger beer in
  two-tone stoneware with "return this bottle" stamped in, soda water.

**Labels (ref).** Plain paper, letterpress in black or one colour on white or buff, framed by a
printer's border, with a stock engraved cut (a still, an eagle, a horse, a distillery from the air,
a Kentucky colonel); the fine and the bitters by **chromolithograph** in full colour with gold. Many
bitters had their name **embossed in the glass** and only a small paper label. A saloon or a grocer
had **its own labels printed** ("Bottled for ..."). Few labels gave a proof; they said "Pure",
"Old", "Sour Mash", "Hand Made", "Copper Distilled", a year.

**Bottles (ref).** Whiskey: amber or clear round quarts ("fifths") with an applied lip; pint and
half-pint flasks (the "union oval", the "coffin") for the pocket. Bitters: square amber, often
embossed; a few figural (a log cabin, a barrel). Gin: the case bottle. Champagne and claret: heavy
dark green, a punt in the bottom, foil. Beer: amber or aqua quarts, a blob top. Ginger beer:
stoneware, tan over cream. Patent medicine: aqua rectangular panels.

## 3. The blind check

A fresh sub-agent that hadn't seen the reasoning judged every name for period feel, wit and
anything modern or real (with web searches): `docs/briefs/booze_check_r1.md`. It found no modern
words and no winks; its catch was real names. **Changed after round 1:** Kessel (Julius Kessler sold
whiskey from Denver to Leadville in exactly these years) to Oberholz, then Brandauer (Oberholz read as Old Overholt); Château Montbrisson (Monbrison is
a real Médoc) to Larcenac; Veuve Lacombe (Champagne Georges Lacombe) to Morandière; Peyrot (a Cognac
house) to Charvet, then Carrière (Chauvet brandy is made in Sonoma); Huber (Joseph Huber Brewing, 1845) to Ebner; Old Medford Rum (Lawrence's trademark,
1871) to Old Newburyport, then Merrimack (Old Newburyport was Caldwell's); Kestler's Bavaria (beside Kessler, and a Dutch beer) to Zeitler's; Hearthstone's
line (Drake's Plantation Bitters' word for word) rewritten; Kilbrannan (a Scottish sound) to Drumbannagh;
Platte River (near Platte Valley) to South Platte; Ezra Tull (a novel's hero) to Tullis; Fothergill's made
an East India Pale Ale; Pelham's given a slogan. Round 2 checked only the new names
(`docs/briefs/booze_check_r2.md`): Dunphy (an Irish whiskey today) became Dunleavy, and the three above
changed again; those four last names had no third round (two rounds a session). Then Sean reads the table (`waiting on Sean` issue) and strikes or changes any name.
